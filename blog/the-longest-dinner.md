# The Longest Dinner I Never Finished

I should say up front that I only remember the first evening because
of the bread. Someone had baked too much of it, on purpose it turned
out, and there was a small argument — friendly, the kind that isn't
really an argument — about whose loaf was better. I was a guest of a
guest, the way you sometimes are at these things, sitting at a long
outdoor table somewhere between the harbour and the church, listening
to a man two seats down explain, patiently and without any apparent
need to convince anyone, why the fish on our plates mattered more
than fish generally does. Good, he kept saying. Clean. Fair. Like a
little liturgy he'd said a thousand times and never tired of.

At some point that evening, almost as an aside, someone mentioned a
truck. A performance, of sorts, that a few people in a town along the
coast had started doing — nothing formal, they said, just a kind of
rehearsal that kept happening. I remember thinking it sounded like
exactly the kind of thing that gets mentioned once at a dinner and
never again. I didn't think about it for another two years.

I only went back to that coast because I'd missed a ferry. That's the
honest version. I had an afternoon to fill in a town I didn't know,
and I wandered into a piazza where a small pickup truck sat with its
tailgate down and a plain table of ordinary things laid out beside
it — plates, an old radio, a child's coat. I recognised none of it as
anything, at first. It was only walking away, an hour later, having
watched a woman trade a length of cloth for a mended bicycle wheel
with no money passing between them at all, that the dinner table came
back to me. Good, clean, fair. I hadn't put those words to a truck
before.

I kept finding excuses to be on that coast after that. Not often —
maybe five or six times over what turned out to be a couple of years
— but enough that I started to recognise faces. The man who always
stood beside whatever was for sale that day, unhurried, the way
you'd stand next to your own garage. The woman who seemed to turn up
whenever there was a promise to witness, nodding slowly at a
blackboard like she was putting her name to something without saying
a word. I never learned most of their names. I'm not sure that
mattered to any of us.

What I noticed, visit after visit, wasn't change so much as
persistence. The same four small movements, roughly — a table, then a
plinth, then a blackboard, then, always, a bottle going round at the
end. Sometimes six people. Once, closer to twenty. Nobody seemed
bothered either way. I asked, once, if it happened every month, and
got a shrug and something like "when it happens, it happens," which I
found maddening at the time and think about fondly now.

The last time I was there, something had changed, though it took me
a while standing in the square to work out what. The truck was in
its usual spot, the blackboard was up, but there was also, running
almost the whole length of one side of the piazza, a long table
covered in food — bread, cheese, bottles of wine and water, dishes I
didn't recognise, all of it simply set down by whoever had carried it
there. Nobody was serving. Nobody seemed to be keeping any kind of
account of who'd brought what, or who was eating whose. People just
arrived, added something or didn't, and ate. I asked a woman standing
near me who had organised the food, half expecting a name, and she
looked at me with something like mild confusion and said nobody had —
it was a spuntino, she said, as if that settled it, which, I
realised, it did.

I understood then what I'd half understood at that first dinner years
before, without knowing I understood it. The fish, the bread, the
argument about whose loaf was better — that same table had simply
kept going, through the truck, through the blackboard, through every
small ordinary transaction I'd half-watched over those years, until
it arrived back at itself: a long table of food nobody was tracking,
in a square nobody was charging admission to, at an event nobody had
scheduled and nobody seemed anxious to schedule again. It didn't feel
like I'd been watching two different things — a dinner once, a
performance several times after. It felt, standing there with a piece
of bread I hadn't paid for and couldn't have paid for if I'd tried,
like I'd been watching one long table the whole time, one that had
simply gone away for a while and come back larger.

I still don't know most of their names. I still don't know when it
will happen again. I've stopped minding not knowing. I think that
might be the whole point — a promise on a blackboard doesn't need a
date, and neither, it turns out, does a table that was never really
laid by any one person to begin with.
