# The False Floor

I went back to that square a third time mostly out of guilt. I'd told
a friend about the truck, the plinth, the blackboard, all of it, with
enough enthusiasm that she'd made a special trip to see it for
herself — and arrived, as it happened, on an evening when almost
nothing occurred at all. Four people at the table. Nothing traded.
One promise chalked up, about a fence that needed mending, and even
that felt more like a passing thought than a ceremony. She was polite
about it, the way you're polite about a restaurant a friend
oversold, but I could tell she thought she'd missed whatever I'd
seen. I felt I owed her a better evening than the one she got.

So I went back a third time, half expecting to have to apologise
again, half hoping for something more like the night with the long
table of food I'd told her about in too much detail. What I got
instead was a Tuesday. Six or seven people. A woman selling a
colander for a jar of something homemade. No blackboard promise at
all that night — the truck's owner just shrugged when I asked, the
way he always does, as if the question itself were slightly beside
the point.

I sat with my coffee and waited for someone to look disappointed.
Nobody did. That was the part that finally got under my skin enough
to ask about properly.

I found the man who always stands near the truck — not the owner, a
different one, older, who I've come to think of as the one who
notices things — and asked him, plainly, whether it bothered anyone
when a night was quiet like this. He looked at me for a second like
I'd asked whether the tide minded going out. He said something I've
turned over many times since: that nobody had ever told him what a
good night was supposed to look like, so a quiet one couldn't very
well disappoint him. I said surely somebody must keep some kind of
score, even privately, even just in their own head. He said maybe
they did, but if so, he'd never met them, and he'd been coming here
a long time.

I pushed a little further, because it seemed important suddenly, and
asked what would actually count as this whole thing failing — the
truck stopping, the table folding up for good, whatever the real
version of it not working would look like. He thought about that
properly, longer than he'd thought about anything else I'd asked
him. Eventually he said the only failure he could imagine was one
Tuesday nobody came back the following month, and then, some
Tuesday after that, nobody remembered why they used to. Everything
short of that, he said — a quiet night, an empty table, a promise
nobody collected on — wasn't failing at anything. It was just
Tuesday.

I told him that seemed a very generous way of scoring things, and he
said he didn't think of it as generous at all. He said most things
in his life had come with someone else's idea of what winning looked
like already bolted on before he'd even decided whether he wanted to
play — a shop needing a certain number of customers a week to count
as worth opening, a garden needing a certain yield to count as worth
keeping, a Sunday football match somehow needing a league table
underneath it before eleven grown men were allowed to just enjoy
kicking a ball around a field. He said somebody, somewhere, always
seemed to arrive first and quietly lay a floor down under a thing,
long before anyone asked whether the thing needed one. Once it was
there, everybody just walked on it without noticing they were
standing on somebody else's numbers.

He said the truck had never had one of those laid under it. Not
because nobody had tried — someone always tries, eventually, usually
with the best intentions, usually with a spreadsheet — but because
by the time they got around to suggesting it, it had already gone on
long enough, quietly enough, that there was nothing underneath left
to attach a floor to. You can't put a threshold under something once
it's already stopped needing one to keep existing.

I didn't have anything to say to that, so I finished my coffee and
watched the woman with the colander walk off with her jar, satisfied
with whatever she'd got, and nobody remotely concerned that the
whole evening might not have amounted to much by anybody else's
count.

On the bench by the fountain sat another old man, there before I arrived
and still there when I left. He hadn't bought anything, hadn't
traded anything, hadn't so much as glanced at the blackboard. He'd
simply watched the square do whatever it was going to do that
evening, with the specific, unhurried attention of someone who has
never once in his life needed a square to do anything in particular
to be worth watching. I found myself wondering, walking past him,
whether a truly empty evening — nobody at all, not even the woman
with the colander — would have troubled him even slightly. I doubt
it would have. I think the only thing capable of moving him off that
bench was his own lunch, at whatever hour lunch happened to arrive,
and nothing the square did or didn't produce in the meantime was
ever going to hurry that along.

Walking back over the bridge afterward, in the last of the light, I
found myself doing the thing he clearly never bothered with — totting
the evening up, trying to decide whether I'd witnessed something or
nothing much. I never did settle it. I've started to think that not
settling it is the nearest thing to an answer I'm going to get, and
that the old man on the bench had settled it decades before I ever
showed up to ask.
