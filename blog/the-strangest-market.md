# The Strangest Market I Never Meant to Find

I wasn't looking for anything. That's the first thing to say, because
it matters later. I'd wandered into the piazza of a small town — the
kind of place where the church bell still runs the day more than any
clock does — looking for a coffee and maybe a shady bench, nothing
more.

There was a small pickup truck parked at one end of the square,
tailgate down, and a handful of things laid out on a plain table.
Nothing special. A stack of plates, a couple of old tools, a
transistor radio, some children's clothes folded in a basket. A man
stood beside it, unhurried, the way you'd stand next to your own
garage on a Sunday. I almost walked past it entirely — it looked like
every clearance sale I'd seen in every town I'd ever passed through.
An older woman stopped, picked up the radio, turned it over in her
hands, and the two of them talked for a minute before she simply put
a few coins on the tailgate and walked off with it. Ordinary. I
ordered my coffee and sat down to watch the square instead, the way
you do when you've got nowhere particular to be.

That's when it started to change, though I didn't understand what I
was watching until much later.

More people arrived — not many, eight or ten, maybe — and the table
itself seemed to rearrange as they did. Objects that had been piled
together a few minutes earlier were suddenly given space of their
own. A small painting went up on what looked like a folding easel.
A carved wooden bowl was set on an upturned crate, alone, like it was
standing on a plinth in a gallery I'd once wandered into in Cagliari.
Nobody announced this. Nobody explained it. It simply happened, the
way a room rearranges itself for a different kind of evening.

And then something genuinely strange: a woman offered a length of
hand-woven cloth, and a man offered her a repaired bicycle wheel in
return. No money changed hands at all. They shook on it, and for a
second — just a second — they both looked at each other in a way I
can only describe as *knowing*. As if something had been agreed
that went beyond the wheel and the cloth. I didn't understand it
then. I think I do a little now.

The last part was the one that stayed with me. A young man, who'd
clearly come with nothing to trade at all, stepped up to a small
blackboard mounted on the side of the truck and, in front of
everyone, chalked up a promise — something about mending a neighbour's
roof before the next full moon, I gathered, though my Italian only
carried me so far. An older woman standing near him nodded slowly,
like she was putting her name to it without saying a word. Nobody
wrote a contract. Nobody took his details. The board just held it,
in plain sight, for anyone to see.

I asked the man near the truck, eventually, what all this was. He
shrugged, smiled, and said something like, "It's just a truck that
parks here sometimes." Which told me nothing and everything at once.

Afterward — and this is the detail I keep turning over — everyone who'd
taken part, buyer, seller, the man with the promise, the woman who'd
witnessed it, simply drifted toward the little bar on the corner and
ordered drinks together, like old friends closing out an ordinary
evening. I was invited along, a total stranger, with no more
introduction than having been sitting there. Nobody asked what I did
for a living. Nobody asked for my number. It didn't feel like I'd
watched a market. It felt like I'd watched something closer to a
performance that had simply forgotten to end.

What struck me most, thinking back on it over the following days,
wasn't any single moment — it was how little of it seemed to be
*run* by anyone at all. No banner, no sign-up sheet, no organiser
working a clipboard. If it happens again next month, it'll happen
because people wanted it to, not because anyone scheduled it. It
could vanish as easily as it appeared, and I got the strong sense
that would be fine too — nothing about it seemed to be straining
toward becoming bigger than it already was. It simply existed, at
whatever size the day brought it, and everyone in that square seemed
completely at ease with not knowing what it would become.

I still don't know what to call it. I'm not sure anyone there did
either. But I've thought about that blackboard more than almost
anything else I saw on the whole trip — a promise, held by nothing
but the people standing around it, with nowhere further to go until
someone comes back to keep it.
