# Understanding Donazione

### A short guide to legal gift-giving in Italy

---

## What it is

Donazione is the legal term for a gift — a contract in which one
party, out of *spirito di liberalità* (a spirit of generosity),
enriches another by transferring a right or taking on an obligation
in their favour, with nothing given in return. It's governed by
Article 769 of the Italian Civil Code, which opens the section of
the code covering gifts.

Unlike permuta, a donazione only moves value in one direction. There's
no counterparty obligation at all — the recipient doesn't have to do
or give anything back for the gift to be valid.

## Two forms, and why the difference matters

Most donations, under Article 782 of the Civil Code and Article 48 of
the Notarial Law, need a formal, solemn act: a notarial deed (*atto
pubblico*), made in front of two witnesses. This isn't a small
formality — skipping it makes the donation void, not just irregular.
The law requires this precisely to make a donor stop and genuinely
reckon with the seriousness of giving something away.

But Article 783 carves out an exception that matters here directly:
**donazione di modico valore** — a gift of modest value. For a gift
like this, none of that formality is needed. The gift is valid the
moment it's handed over — even a purely symbolic handover counts —
with no deed, no witnesses, no notary. This is sometimes called
*donazione manuale*: a gift completed simply by giving.

What counts as "modest" isn't a fixed number written anywhere in the
law. Courts have held that it has to be judged relative to the
donor's own financial situation, not against some absolute price tag —
the same case-by-case, no-fixed-threshold approach already used for
abitualità. A gift that barely registers against one person's means
might be significant against another's; what matters is whether it
noticeably affects the giver's own wealth, not the object's market
price in isolation.

## Liberalità d'uso: gifts that aren't really "donations" at all

Article 809 extends the donation rules, where compatible, to other
acts of generosity too — but explicitly carves out anything motivated
by **dovere morale o obbligo naturale**: moral duty or social custom.
A birthday present, a wedding gift, a token brought to a gathering —
these are generally treated as *liberalità d'uso*, customary
generosity, sitting outside the donation framework altogether rather
than merely qualifying for its "modest value" exception. The law
recognises that not every act of giving is really a donation in the
formal sense; some of it is just how people behave with each other.

## Does giving repeatedly ever become a business?

Permuta and vendita both carry a real abitualità risk — repeated,
organised activity can tip an occasional private exchange into
something requiring a VAT number. A pure gift is a different case:
since nothing is received in return, there's no revenue and no
consideration changing hands for tax law to fasten onto. Giving things
away, even often, isn't the same kind of activity a business licence
exists to capture.

That said, the same underlying question — is this occasional and
personal, or organised and repeated in a way that starts looking like
something else entirely — is still worth keeping in mind, for the
same reason it matters everywhere else: it's less about any single
gift's value, and more about the pattern the giving forms over time.

## In short

- Donazione is a gift, one-directional: nothing is owed back.
- Most donations need a notarial deed and two witnesses — skipping
  this makes the gift void.
- A gift of modest value is exempt from that formality entirely — it's
  complete the moment it's handed over.
- What counts as "modest" is judged against the giver's own means, not
  a fixed price, the same case-by-case approach as abitualità.
- Customary gifts — birthdays, gatherings, social occasions — often
  sit outside the donation framework altogether, as *liberalità d'uso*.
- A pure gift carries none of permuta's conguaglio risk, since no
  money and no counter-obligation are ever involved.

Donazione isn't a loophole either. It's simply what the law calls
something people have always done — give something away, freely,
because they chose to — and it asks for almost nothing in return for
that choice to be respected.
