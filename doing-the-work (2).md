# Doing the Work

Nobody learns to ride a bicycle by being told how. You can describe
the physics precisely — the gyroscopic effect of the spinning wheels,
the constant small corrections that keep a moving bike upright when a
stationary one falls over instantly — and the description changes
nothing about whether the person hearing it can actually ride. The
only way to acquire that particular knowledge is to get on, fall off,
and correct, over and over, until the body knows something the mind
was never able to fully put into words in the first place.

The philosopher Michael Polanyi built an entire account of knowledge
around exactly this observation, and gave it a name worth borrowing:
**tacit knowledge** — captured in his own short phrase, *we can know
more than we can tell*. Some things genuinely cannot be transmitted by
explanation, no matter how good the explanation is. They can only be
acquired by doing the thing itself, personally, and there is no
shortcut that produces the same result.

## What this means for anything built to be understood

Most things that claim to teach quietly assume the opposite —
that understanding is a payload, something that can be packaged into
an explanation and handed across cleanly, the same shape on both
ends. Tacit knowledge says this is only sometimes true, and it's
never true of the things that matter most. A manual can tell you how
a bicycle works. It cannot make you able to ride one. Somewhere
between "explained" and "understood," for a real class of things,
there is a gap that only doing the work yourself can close.

This creates an uncomfortable but honest design choice for anything
built around a skill or a discipline rather than a fact. You can
optimise for ease of explanation — make everything as clear, as
accessible, as fully described as possible — or you can accept that
some understanding will only ever belong to the people who actually
did the thing, repeatedly, and let the explanation only ever be a
door rather than a destination. These pull against each other. Past a
point, more explanation doesn't produce more understanding; it just
produces more people who can repeat the explanation without having
done the work it was describing.

## The cost, honestly

Choosing the second path has a real price, and it's worth naming
rather than dressing up as something else. It is genuinely
exclusionary — to anyone unwilling or unable to put in the repeated
effort, to anyone who only ever wants the summary. Someone who reads
every word carefully but never does the thing itself will still not
fully have it, and no amount of better writing closes that gap,
because the gap was never made of words in the first place.

That cost is worth accepting on purpose rather than pretending it
isn't there. The alternative — explaining everything so thoroughly
that nobody ever has to verify or practice anything themselves —
doesn't actually produce more understanding. It produces the
appearance of understanding, at a much lower price, which is a
different and considerably weaker thing wearing the same clothes.

Traditional craft apprenticeship has always worked this way, long
before anyone called it tacit knowledge: a trade was never fully
transmitted through written instruction, only through years spent
beside someone who already had it, doing the motions themselves until
the body learned what the words alone never could. Open, verify-it-
yourself cultures — in software, in finance, in any community that
insists on "trust, but check" rather than "trust me" — are simply
running the same principle in a different domain: nobody gets to
outsource the checking and still claim the understanding. A
performer's sense of comic timing belongs to the same family, and may
be the plainest example of all: no script or coaching note can specify
the exact half-second a line needs to land, only repetition in front
of a real, unpredictable room, night after night, until the body
simply knows.

Nobody is owed comprehension of a thing like this. They're owed a
door, genuinely open — and the work on the other side of it has
always belonged to whoever chooses to walk through.
