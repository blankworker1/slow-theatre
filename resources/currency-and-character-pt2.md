# Currency and Character pt2

There is another way to run the same two devices, and it produces
the opposite result.

Take the traditional stage as the model rather than the exception.
A performer takes on a role, and for as long as the performance
lasts, the role is what's seen and judged, not the person underneath
it. This isn't dishonest — everyone in the room knows it's a
performance, the curtain will fall, and the actor will step back
into themselves the moment it does. The device works because it's
bounded. It has a beginning, an end, and an audience that knows
which is which.

Now remove the boundary, and keep everything else.

Picture a role performed continuously, with no curtain and no
interval — a public character, worn every day, in full view, doing
the ordinary things a law-abiding person does. Underneath it, on a
separate and unlit stage, the actual business of a life is
conducted: money changes hands, work is done, value moves — but
off the record the visible character belongs to. The two aren't
alternating scenes in the same play. They're two performances
running at once, and the entire function of the visible one is to
make sure no audience is ever in the room for the other.

This is the same pairing as before — a character worn to remain
unanswerable, a currency moved to remain unaccountable — but the
relationship between the two has inverted. In the bounded version,
currency and character are each, separately, a substitute for
presence: a price stands in for a negotiation, a mask stands in for
a person. Here they've stopped being separate devices doing similar
jobs, and become one device with two parts, each protecting the
other. The visible character exists to provide cover for the
currency that has to stay hidden; the hidden currency exists because
the visible character has made honesty about it unthinkable. Neither
would need to exist without the other holding the curtain shut.

And this is where the traditional stage's one honest feature quietly
disappears. A real performance ends. The mask comes off, the actor
takes a bow as themselves, and the audience applauds the person, not
the role — that moment of recognition is built into the form. A
performance with no curtain has no such moment, structurally,
anywhere in it. The person wearing the public character and the
person conducting the private business are never required to occupy
the same scene, at the same time, in front of the same audience.
There is no interval in which the two are compared, no mirror held
up in which both could be seen at once. Not concealment, exactly —
concealment implies something being actively hidden from a witness
who's looking. This is closer to a performance with no witness
scheduled at all: nobody is confronted, because a confrontation
requires two things to be in the same room together, and the whole
arrangement's only real function is making sure they never are.

Seen this way, it isn't really a story about money, or about the
law, or even about honesty in the ordinary sense. It's a much
older story about what happens when a society keeps theatre's
oldest device — the mask that absorbs a person's exposure — and
never asks the one question that would end it: when does the
curtain fall? A performer who never leaves the stage isn't
performing anymore. They've simply stopped having a self the
audience is permitted to see, and, after long enough, may have
stopped fully seeing it themselves.

But the bounded version, held up earlier as the honest contrast, isn't
as clean as it first looked either. A curtain falling doesn't return
a person to themselves automatically — it returns them to an
audience, one more time, and what's savored in that last moment
isn't always the self stepping free of the role. It can just as
easily be the applause: recognition sought, weighed, and enjoyed,
for having worn the mask well. That's a third thing, distinct from
both character and currency, and quieter than either. The ego
doesn't need a role to hide behind or a price to hide inside; it
only needs to be seen a certain way, and rewarded for it. Applause
is itself a kind of currency — paid out and received in an instant,
discharging nothing but the desire that asked for it. A person can
drop every external device, keep no character and take no price,
and still be performing, privately, for exactly this: not the
audience's judgment of a role, but its judgment of them.

This is why removing currency and character was never, on its own,
the resolution. It empties the two obvious hiding places and leaves
the oldest one standing — the wish to be well thought of, which no
curtain, real or absent, closes by itself. That wish has to be seen
for what it is before anything else clears: also staged, also a
transaction, the self doing the savoring still performing even in
the moment it believes the performance has ended. A person who has
dropped the mask but not the hunger for the room's approval has only
found a subtler stage to stand on — and until that stage is
recognized as a stage too, there is no ground underneath any of this
that isn't still, in some quiet way, a performance.
