# Understanding Permuta

### A short guide to legal barter in Italy

---

## What it is

Permuta is the direct exchange of one thing for another — no money
changes hands, just two goods changing owners at the same moment.
It's governed by Articles 1552-1555 of the Italian Civil Code, and
is, in essence, the modern, legally recognized form of what's always
simply been called barter.

No written document is required. No notary is needed, except for
real estate or registered movable property. All it takes is
agreement between two people and the mutual handover of what they're
exchanging.

## One word, not two

In a sale there's a seller and a buyer — two different roles, because
there are two different obligations: one party has to hand over a
good, the other has to pay a sum of money. In permuta, this
distinction doesn't exist. Both parties do exactly the same thing —
they each give up a good — so the law calls them by the same name:
**permutanti**. Nobody buys, nobody sells. Two people exchange
something, on equal footing.

## Why the state watches the money, not the objects

Most of the rules governing the economy — the cash transaction cap
(€5,000), banks' anti-money-laundering reports, the requirement for
a VAT number, the new rules for online platforms like Vinted or
eBay — exist to track money as it moves. They're tools built to
intercept a monetary flow.

A pure permuta — thing for thing, with not a euro involved — doesn't
generate any flow of that kind. It's not that the law specifically
exempts barter: it's that barter simply doesn't produce the one
thing these tools are built to watch for.

## The conguaglio: when a little cash enters the picture

Sometimes an exchange isn't perfectly balanced, and one party adds a
sum of money to even out the difference — this is called a
**conguaglio** (a cash top-up). As long as that sum clearly remains
smaller than the value of the good being exchanged, the contract
stays a permuta in every legal sense. But if the money becomes the
larger part of the deal, case law tends to treat it as a sale, with
the other good counted as a partial payment in kind — the same way
trading in your old car plus a sum of money to buy a new one from a
dealer works.

## Abitualità: the real dividing line

Whether it's a permuta or simply an occasional sale of your own used
belongings, the question that actually matters legally is never "how
much" but "how often, and how organized." An activity becomes a
business — requiring a VAT number — when it's **abitualità**
(habitual): stable over time, repeated with organization. An
occasional exchange between private individuals, even if repeated a
few times, stays an exchange between private individuals. There's no
fixed number of times per year that triggers the obligation — it's a
case-by-case assessment, based on how stable and systematic the
activity is, not on a threshold written down anywhere.

## In short

- Permuta is thing-for-thing, no money: no document required, no
  VAT number.
- Both parties are called permutanti — there's no seller and no
  buyer.
- The state's rules follow the money, not the objects: pure barter
  leaves nothing to follow.
- A cash conguaglio still counts as permuta as long as it's clearly
  the smaller part of the exchange.
- What matters is abitualità — regularity and organization over
  time — not the size of any single exchange.

Permuta isn't some hidden loophole in the law. It's simply the
oldest exchange there is, still perfectly legal, because it never
needed to be anything else.
