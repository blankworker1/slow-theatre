# Il lupo perde il pelo ma non il vizio

*Literally: "The wolf sheds its coat, but not the vice." The
Italian equivalent of "a leopard can't change its spots" — except
where the English proverb is about character, the Italian one is
about *vizio*, a habit, a vice — something closer to compulsion
than identity. That difference is the whole document.*

## Why this proverb

Every year, the wolf sheds its coat. Underneath, it's the same
animal — the *vizio*, the proverb says, never really leaves. It's a
saying about character that doesn't change. For La Prova, we take it
literally, in the opposite direction: for one hour, the coat comes
off.

Nobody — performer or visitor — is asked to become a different
person. Only to set down, for the length of one rehearsal, one
specific habit: thinking in terms of price, of purchase, of "how
much." That's a vice in the plainest sense of the word — an
automatic reflex, not a considered choice. And like any vice, it can
be paused, even for just an afternoon. The coat grows back. The wolf
stays a wolf. But for one hour, it isn't there.

This document explains why that gesture — pausing the vice of money
— sits at the centre of La Prova, what actually happens during the
performance, and how we explain it to performers and visitors alike.

## The shape of La Prova

La Prova runs in three acts, with no curtain and no announced start:
whoever arrives already knows the show has begun.

**Act One — L'Usato.** A table, simple, like a market stall. Objects
from the cellar, with no price. Whoever takes something decides
alone whether it's useful at home, whether it could become material
for a piece, or whether it's worth reselling elsewhere. No exchange
is required: it's pure gift.

**Act Two — L'Arte.** The same objects, transformed or standing
alongside new works, appear on plinths and easels — the staging
itself signals "this is art," with no label needed. Here, exchange
happens: art for art, object for object, never for money.

**Act Three — La Promessa.** A blackboard is mounted on the side of
the truck. Under the painted heading "OFFERTE E FAVORI," whoever has
nothing to exchange right now can leave a promise instead — written,
dated, seen by the community present. No outside authority
guarantees it; it's witnessed, not certified.

The truck leaves the way it arrived, with no formal close to the
scene — a shared toast dissolves the line between who was performing
and who was watching, and everyone simply goes back to being
themselves.

## The three boundaries

La Prova is held inside three overlapping boundaries, each of a
different nature.

**The physical boundary.** The radius of the signal — the perimeter
covered by the truck's network (BosaTAZ). Whoever is inside the
signal is inside the zone; whoever is outside, isn't. No sign
declares it: the phone does that on its own, announcing "no internet
connection."

**The fiscal boundary.** The legal perimeter: only barter and gift,
never a sale, never a cash top-up. This is the boundary that has to
hold up in front of an official or an inspection — which is why it's
written in the most precise, verifiable language of the three.

**The mental boundary.** The least visible, and the one that makes
the other two real. This is the boundary of behaviour: inside the
zone, the very idea of "buying" shouldn't even surface as an
available thought. A truck with no network and a law about barter
can both be true, and someone inside the perimeter can still be
thinking like they're at the supermarket. The mental boundary is
what actually closes this space off — and it's simply the vice that
needs to be paused.

Performers are no exception. La Prova doesn't ask them to *portray*
the absence of money while quietly working around it — no requested
donations, no hat passed at the end. The mental boundary applies to
whoever is performing exactly as much as to whoever is watching: the
performance demonstrates the rule because it lives inside it, not
because it narrates it.

## For performers

> Money is a vice, not a need. Somewhere in this hour, your hand
> will want to reach for a price, a coin, a "how much." That's just
> the vice talking — the muscle memory of every transaction you've
> ever made in your life. The scene doesn't ask you to resist it
> dramatically. It only asks you to... not reach. Let the impulse
> pass, the way you'd let the urge to check your phone pass.

## For the aspetta-tori

> You're in a place with no prices. For a moment that might feel
> strange — like patting your pocket for your phone while you're
> already holding it. That's the vice of money, and it's the one
> thing not welcome here. Everything else — talking, exchanging,
> giving, asking — works exactly the way it always does.
