# Slow Theatre — Exercises

*A growing companion to the philosophical documents — not
explanations of the structure, but small, optional, bounded
rehearsals of one specific reflex at a time, practiced before the
stakes are real. General to Slow Theatre, not tied to any one
Node — RESERVATO, for instance, is useful anywhere a gift table
exists, not only at BosaTAZ. Il lupo perde il pelo ma non il vizio
names the vice and tells you to let the impulse pass. An exercise is
the thing you actually do to build the muscle that instruction
describes.*

## RESERVATO

**The convention.** A small sticker, placed on an object, reading
simply RESERVATO. No sign explains it, no performer announces it,
and none of that is needed — everyone already carries the same
reflex from ordinary life. A restaurant table marked *riservato*,
empty and visibly available, still gets left alone. Nobody flags
down the waiter to argue. Nobody asks why. The marker alone does
all the work a lock or a bouncer would otherwise have to do. This
exercise imports that exact convention, unchanged, into Act One.

**Two lessons, one sticker.**

*For the Donatore, it's agency without justification.* This
agency already exists in Act One's own rule — the giver decides,
privately, full stop — it just usually stays invisible: someone
simply doesn't put an object out, or passes it to a friend quietly,
and no one else is any the wiser. RESERVATO doesn't grant new
power. It makes existing, private discretion visible for a moment,
without converting it into something that then has to be defended
because it's now public. That's the genuinely useful part: making a
boundary visible usually invites scrutiny of it. This convention is
one of the rare cases where visibility and zero justification
coexist without friction.

*For the aspetta-tore, it's the harder half.* Wanting something,
seeing it sitting right there, unclaimed, reachable — and simply
not reaching, not asking why, not turning someone else's private
choice into a problem for them to solve. Where the money-vice is a
compulsion to price things, this is a compulsion to feel owed an
explanation for someone else's line. The exercise is the same
shape as the wolf/vice document's core instruction, aimed at a
different reflex: not "don't reach for a price," but "don't reach
for a reason."

**A third, practical use, requiring no separate convention.** The
same sticker, same expected response, also protects whatever's
present at a rehearsal but was never part of the gift table at
all — tools, equipment, the truck's own kit, anything sharing the
same open, unguarded space as L'Usato's objects without actually
being offered. An aspetta-tore trained on the first meaning behaves
correctly around the second without ever being told there's a
difference. The lesson was never "understand why a thing is
reserved." It was "don't need to" — which turns out to cover both
cases with the same sticker, the same silence, and the same
respected boundary.

**Using it well.** RESERVATO stays an occasional, deliberate choice,
not a default. Act One's anonymity — nobody chosen, nobody
unchosen, to take part — is what keeps its threshold as low as it
is; if reservation became the norm rather than the exception, Act
One would start quietly importing Act Three's selectivity without
Act Three's safeguard (a promise is witnessed and accountable
precisely because it's directed at someone by name; a reserved
object, used too often, would be just as directed with none of
that accountability behind it). Used sparingly, on purpose, it
stays a rehearsal tool. Used constantly, it would quietly become a
different table.

## Keep Up the Act

**A different kind of act than it sounds.** In the ordinary sense,
"keeping up an act" means sustaining a fiction — hiding a real self
behind a false one, the exact device Currency and Character argues
Slow Theatre exists to strip away. This exercise isn't that.
Nobody performing it pretends to be anyone other than themselves,
and nothing about what they're doing is invented: the giving is
real, the transformation is real, the promise is real. What's being
kept up isn't a false identity — it's the *ordinariness* of the
scene, for as long as it needs to hold, so the performance stays
invisible to whoever hasn't crossed into it yet.

**Why this needs practicing at all.** The whole low-threshold design
depends on nobody announcing what's happening. A stranger wandering
up to a table sees objects, not a performance — and that has to stay
true right up until they choose, on their own, to take something.
The instant somebody tells them "actually, this is a piece called
La Prova, and..." the threshold isn't low anymore. It's been
explained into existence, which is the one thing the whole design
was built to avoid.

**Two reflexes this trains against, and the second is the sharper
one.**

*The urge to explain.* Confusion in a stranger's face is
uncomfortable, and the kindest, most natural response is to resolve
it — to tell them what's really going on. That instinct has to be
resisted here, gently and without coldness: an unexplained object on
a table is still just an object on a table, and staying with that
plainness, rather than relieving it, is the exercise.

*The urge for credit.* This is the same reflex Currency and Character
names as the third, quieter layer underneath money and mask — not
wanting to be paid, but wanting to be *recognized*. "Let me tell you
what you're actually witnessing" is applause sought in disguise,
dressed up as generosity toward a confused visitor. Resisting it
means letting an object simply change hands, unremarked, with no
credit claimed for the fact that something unusual just occurred.

**Using it well.** Like RESERVATO, this isn't asking anyone to be
cold or evasive if genuinely asked a direct question — someone who
asks "what is this?" can be told plainly. The exercise is about not
*volunteering* the explanation first, and not needing the stranger
to know something remarkable just happened in order for it to have
been worth doing. The performance staying invisible to itself isn't
a trick being played on visitors. It's the same discipline as
letting an impulse toward a price pass, aimed instead at an impulse
toward recognition.

**Worked example — the three questions a Donatore will actually
hear.** These come up often enough to be worth having a real answer
ready, rather than improvising one under a stranger's gaze:

- *"Why are you giving this away for free?"* → "I don't need it
  anymore — someone else might." True, ordinary, and complete. No
  mention of La Prova is needed, because it isn't the true reason —
  it's simply not what's being asked.
- *"Are you sure you don't want something for this?"* → "No,
  honestly, it's fine." Same principle: the plain truth already
  answers it.
- *"I want to pay."* → "No, please — it's a gift." This one isn't
  really about keeping up the act at all; it's the no-conguaglio
  rule from bosataz-fiscal.md arriving as a live moment instead of a
  clause. Accepting payment here wouldn't just change the tone, it
  would legally convert a donazione into a sale. But the refusal
  still needs no legal explanation — it's exactly how anyone declines
  an unwanted repayment anywhere, gift or otherwise. The law is
  doing its work quietly underneath the ordinary politeness, not
  spoken aloud.

None of these three answers reveal anything. That's the whole
point: the true answer and the undisclosed one are the same
sentence.

**At scale, the same trick gets harder, and it's worth admitting
that rather than pretending otherwise.** One Donatore clearing a
cellar looks like nothing in particular. Many mats and many
pedestals, running in shifts around a truck, visibly look
organized — because they are. "Who organized this?" stops being
idle curiosity at that size; it's the correct question, and
answering it with a deflection would stop being *not volunteering*
and start being simply false. The honest version, straight from the
Handbook rather than invented for the occasion, is that there
isn't an organizer in the sense the question usually means — no
company, no producer, nobody with authority over anyone else's
part in it. "A few of us agreed to do this together" is true,
ordinary in register even though the arrangement behind it isn't,
and reveals nothing that functions as a name or a pitch. "Why are
you here?" doesn't get harder at scale the same way — a person's
own reason for showing up stays answerable regardless of how large
the rehearsal has grown.

For whoever pushes past that and genuinely wants the full account —
the real structure, the real way in — the Participate tab's QR
code is exactly the release valve for this moment specifically.
Point them at it rather than explaining further out loud. The
complete picture exists; it's just never delivered standing at the
mats. It's found later, on their own terms, off the clock.

**The universal fallback, underneath every exercise above.** Anyone,
in any role, facing any question at any point in any conversation,
can simply point to the Participate tab's QR code rather than
constructing an answer on the spot. It requires no judgment call
about how much to say, no read on a stranger's tone, nothing
memorized. This isn't itself an exercise — it's what makes every
exercise above safe to attempt imperfectly, because there's always
a zero-effort way out that reveals nothing and explains everything,
just never out loud.
