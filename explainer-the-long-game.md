# THE LONG GAME — Performance Structure Explainer

*A working reference for scripting. This is architecture, not script — it describes how the piece is built so that dialogue, backstories, and staging can be written consistently on top of it.*

---

## What kind of piece this is

The Long Game is staged as a fictional public meeting, performed as **invisible theatre** and **method acting**: bystanders are not told in advance that they're watching a performance. Locals will recognize that the "mayor" is not the real mayor — for them, the fiction is transparent from the start, and the pleasure/discomfort is in watching real local tensions performed back at them. For tourists and outsiders, the meeting reads as real for as long as the piece allows it to.

The subject matter is a real, live local issue (most likely a wind farm), but the specific topic is deliberately withheld from publicity beforehand — exactly as a real public meeting works. Nobody arrives already knowing what's on the agenda.

This is a significant step up in complexity from La Prova, because it is layered — every character exists at three depths at once, and the show's plot is generated by *live decisions* (who speaks, who's cut off, what the vote produces), not by a fixed scene order. It needs to be scripted carefully precisely because so much of it has to work whether or not the "unscripted" moments happen.

---

## The four layers

### 1. Position (fixed — 3 total)

The invisible logic underneath everything. Every character on stage holds one of three positions on what should happen to the land:

- **Endure** — things should stay as they are (the never-left)
- **Stop it** — active resistance to speculation and tourism-driven change (the returned / SURRA)
- **Develop it** — infrastructure, tourism, and investment are the way forward (the speculator)

Positions are never announced or named on stage. The audience should never hear the word "position" — they should only ever meet roles and voices.

### 2. Role (variable — costumes on a position)

A role is a specific character type built on top of a position. Each role contains a built-in mismatch between what it says and what it actually does or represents — this is where the piece's irony lives.

| Position | Roles | The mismatch |
|---|---|---|
| Endure | The never-left (e.g. the mother) | Resignation performed/read as peace |
| Endure | Holiday-home owner | Loves the picturesque village, has no idea a former resident's home now sits empty because of them |
| Stop it | SURRA (returned generation) | Genuine care for the land can slide into ethnonationalist language; offers inclusion to others while excluding by instinct |
| Develop it | The speculator | Milan/Rome accent, capital logic, no local pretense needed |
| Develop it | The mayor | Sincere "jobs" argument, personally took backhanders to pass planning permission — and doubles as chair/narrator (see Frame) |

Roles are not required to appear in pairs — but where a position carries more than one role, the roles should never agree on *how* to pursue their shared solution, even though they agree on *what* the solution is. That disagreement is where comedy tends to live (two "develop it" roles bickering about method) and where pathos tends to live (two "endure" roles who both stay, for entirely different reasons).

### 3. Voice (multiple per role — individuals)

A voice is an actual person: a specific backstory, not a type. Multiple voices can share a role. They are written as genuine individuals, each internally coherent, who happen to be at odds with each other **without realizing it** — the disagreement should never be staged as an argument between them; it should simply be visible to the audience in what each one says and doesn't say.

Backstory at this layer only needs to supply the one or two facts that explain why a voice uses their role's language the way they do. Example: two "never-left" voices might both "endure," but one because a parent died on the island last year and grief has hardened into resignation, the other because grandchildren still visit every summer and the future still feels intact to them. Neither is wrong. They would likely never argue if seated together — they simply want different things while using the same words.

### 4. Frame (how the whole thing is staged and run)

This is the layer that makes the piece function as invisible theatre and as a public meeting simultaneously.

**Opening**
- The mayor (as chair) announces the rules of the meeting at the start.
- The mayor then invites the speculator up, informally introduced as the "guest of honor" (not a literal title).
- From here, the meeting follows a pre-agreed running order.

**Running order = plot**
- There are no acts. Instead, actors are added to the timeline one at a time, in a pre-agreed sequence, functioning like a real meeting's list of speakers.
- The mayor-as-chair has discretion to take comments from the audience at any point — this discretionary power is never explicitly stated at the start; it simply gets used.
- Pacing is controlled independently of content: how long the chair lets someone run, whether they're cut off, whether they're skipped, is itself part of what the piece is saying about power, without ever saying it directly.

**The mayor's dual function**
- The mayor is the one character whose function shifts depending on what's being delivered around him — sometimes chair, sometimes participant, sometimes (at the very end) narrator.
- At the close, the mayor shifts into a different voice entirely, stepping outside the fiction as narrator, to tell the audience plainly: this was theatre, but the subject matter is real.

**The vote**
- The narrator then calls for a show of hands.
- Counting is built to allow different outcomes at different performances — including the mayor declaring, as narrator, that the decision is actually outside his hands (echoing the real dynamic where "strategic infrastructure" decisions sit above the local level).

**Audience**
- Standing and sitting, deliberately mirroring the fact that real public meetings never have enough seats.
- One or more roles (e.g. the mother) may be planted physically within the crowd rather than staged apart from it — but this only changes *where* an already-scripted moment is delivered from. It is not itself unscripted.
- The one genuinely unscripted element in the whole piece: if and when someone from the real audience wants to speak. The mayor decides, in the moment, whether to allow it. Everything else in the frame is scripted but variable in outcome; this alone is genuinely open.

---

## Why this needs careful scripting

Because the plot is generated by decisions (speaking order, cut-offs, who's allowed to speak, how the vote is counted) rather than by a fixed sequence of scenes, every voice's material has to work correctly regardless of:
- how long they're given
- whether they're interrupted
- whether the vote goes one way or another
- whether a real bystander ever actually speaks up

That means backstories and lines need to be written to hold their shape in fragments, not just as a complete monologue — because on any given night, a voice might only get half of what they were written to say.
