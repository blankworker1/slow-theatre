# Applied Economic Agnosticism

2026-09-18 · @Someone

## Abstract

This paper introduces *belief-dependency* as an analytic axis measuring the degree to which a transaction's completeness is outsourced to a party not present at the exchange — an issuer, an institution, a protocol, or a future counterparty. We show that barter, correctly understood, is not an anthropological precursor to money but a structurally distinct mode of exchange characterized by zero belief-dependency, available at any point in economic history including the present. We argue that belief-dependency in monetary systems functions as a substitute for the direct observation of *economic entropy* — the tendency of value, condition, or existence to decay or become uncertain across time and distance — and that monetary crises such as hyperinflation are best understood as the discharge of entropy that belief-dependent accounting had ceased to track. From this we derive *applied economic agnosticism*: not a normative preference for barter over money, but a recognition capacity — the ability to identify, for any transaction, whether and how much of its completeness depends on an absent party, and whether the entropy that dependency conceals is in principle observable. We show that apparent limits on agnostic exchange — storage over time, coordination at scale, and specialization — collapse to a single boundary condition: separation of the transacting parties by time or distance from one another and from the entropy governing what they exchange.

## 1. Introduction

The standard account of monetary history is sequential: barter first, money second, each later instrument an efficiency gain on the one before it. Adam Smith's version of this sequence — direct exchange giving way to a common medium once the "double coincidence of wants" proved too costly — remains the implicit backdrop of most introductory treatments of money, and of much of the theoretical literature built on top of it.

The anthropological record does not support this sequence. Graeber (2011) finds no ethnographic evidence of a society organized around barter as a baseline mode of exchange prior to money; what precedes coinage in the historical record is instead informal social credit — reciprocal, memory-based obligation within a bounded community — and, independently, direct trade of physical goods between parties without ongoing relationship, typically strangers, enemies, or participants in a collapsed monetary system. Both predate and postdate coinage. Neither is superseded by it.

This paper proceeds from that correction to a broader claim. The relevant distinction between money and barter was never chronological. It is what each requires in order to complete: money requires belief — in an issuer, an institution, or a protocol not present at the transaction — while direct barter requires none. Harari's (2011) observation that money is the most successful shared fiction in history is best read not as a description of money's origin but of its *mechanism*: money works because, and only because, a sufficient number of absent parties keep believing in it. Barter's mechanism is different in kind, not merely in scale, and that difference — belief-dependency — is the subject of this paper.

Section 2 defines belief-dependency formally. Section 3 distinguishes three registers in which it occurs. Section 4 revisits the barter/money relationship under this framework. Sections 5 and 6 examine the institutional and thermodynamic substrates of belief-dependency respectively. Section 7 derives a single boundary condition from what initially appear to be three separate limits on agnostic exchange; Annex A develops this into a break-even model comparing direct exchange and belief-dependent settlement for the same transaction. Section 8 sets out applied economic agnosticism as a method of recognition rather than a prescription. Section 9 relates the framework to existing monetary theory, in particular chartalism and Modern Monetary Theory. Section 10 concludes.

## 2. Definitions: The Belief-Dependency Axis

**Definition 1 (Belief-dependency).** A transaction's belief-dependency is the degree to which its completeness — the certainty that both parties receive what they were promised — relies on a party not present at the exchange. An absent party may be an issuer, a bank, a court, a regulator, or a decentralized protocol's continued operation.

**Definition 2 (Economic agnosticism).** A transaction is economically agnostic to the extent that its completeness requires no absent party. Full agnosticism is achieved when settlement is total, immediate, and verifiable by the parties present, using nothing that requires trust in anyone who is not.

These are matters of degree, not a binary. A direct barter of goods with immediate use value sits at the fully agnostic end. A promise to deliver value after a delay, redeemable only through a third party's continued willingness to honor it, sits at the fully belief-dependent end. Most real transactions fall between the two.

## 3. Three Registers of Belief-Dependency

Belief-dependency is not uniform in kind. We distinguish three registers, ordered from thickest to thinnest reliance on an absent party.

| Register | What is trusted | Example | Belief thickness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Institutional | A named, specific authority | Central bank, commercial bank, notary, court | Thick — a single identifiable party, replaceable in principle, can fail or default |
| Protocol | A decentralized system's continued, correct operation, with no single party to appeal to | A public, permissionless blockchain network | Thin — distributed, but not zero: requires the network to keep existing and behaving consistently |
| None | Nothing beyond what is directly observable by the parties present | Direct barter of a physical good with immediate use value | Zero |

The distinction between the institutional and protocol registers matters because they are often conflated in discussions of "trustless" systems. A cryptographically verified bearer instrument removes the institutional register entirely — no bank, no notary, no mint is required to check its authenticity — which is a genuine and significant reduction in belief-dependency relative to fiat instruments. It does not, however, reach the zero point. A holder still depends on the protocol continuing to be run, correctly, by a sufficient number of other parties, none of whom are present at any given exchange. This is a thinner belief than institutional trust, and importantly a different kind of belief — distributed rather than concentrated, difficult to coerce rather than impossible to fail — but it remains a register of belief-dependency, not an exit from the axis. Only the third register, direct barter of goods verifiable on the spot, reaches zero.

## 4. Barter Reconsidered: Agnosticism, Not Origin

Under Definitions 1 and 2, barter is not a primitive stage superseded by money. It is the zero-point of the belief-dependency axis, reachable at any moment in economic history, including the present, whenever two parties settle a trade of immediately useful goods on the spot.

This has an important consequence for the historical record. What Graeber identifies as predating coinage — informal, reciprocal social credit within a bounded community — is not agnostic in the sense defined here. It is itself belief-dependent, at village scale: it requires the lender to believe the borrower will eventually reciprocate, and the community's shared memory functions as an early, informal institutional register. "I owe you one" is a belief system in miniature, not an absence of one.

Genuine zero-register barter, by contrast, is documented mainly at the margins: between strangers or hostile parties with no shared credit relationship to draw on, or in the aftermath of a monetary collapse, where an existing belief-dependent system has failed and reversion to direct trade is the only remaining option. These are not failures of barter. They are exactly the conditions under which belief-dependency cannot be sustained, and the zero-register option becomes the only one still available.

The implication is that barter's frequency in economic life has varied enormously across history, but its *requirements* have not. It was never waiting to be improved upon by money; it occupies a different point on an axis that money does not traverse. Money's expansion did not eliminate the zero-belief option. It simply made the alternative — thick institutional belief-dependency — sufficiently convenient that the zero-belief option came to be used rarely, and, we argue in Section 8, came to be recognized rarely as well.

## 5. Mediated Belief and the Institutional Apparatus

Belief-dependency is not self-executing. A shared conviction that a token carries value does not, on its own, resolve a dispute, detect a counterfeit, or enforce a broken promise. It requires administration — a class of intermediaries whose function is to guarantee what the token cannot guarantee unassisted. Historically this class has taken the form of scribes and temple treasuries, mints and assayers, and later bankers, notaries, regulators, auditors, and central banks. The form changes; the function — validating, enforcing, and where necessary sanctifying an exchange that the two parties could not fully trust each other to complete unaided — does not.

This generates a structural claim rather than an incidental observation: money and its administering class are not two separable requirements but one. A currency with no institution willing to stand behind it is not a weaker currency; under the definitions used here, it has exited the institutional register entirely and is either a protocol-register asset or ceases to function as money at all. Every institutional-register belief system in the historical record has arrived bundled with its own class of administrators.

Barter, at the zero register, requires no equivalent class. No scribe records a direct trade of goods for immediate use; no notary certifies it; no auditor reviews it afterward. The decision of whether the trade is fair is made entirely by the two parties present, using only what each can observe directly. This is the practical content of zero belief-dependency: not merely the absence of a token, but the absence of the entire administrative apparatus a token's value requires.

## 6. Entropy as the Substrate of Belief

We use *economic entropy* to denote the tendency of any fact relevant to an exchange — an asset's condition, its scarcity, its continued existence, its issuer's solvency — to change, decay, or become uncertain as it is followed across time and distance. This is a deliberate extension of the thermodynamic and information-theoretic senses of the term: physical goods decay (thermodynamic entropy), and the future state of any system not directly observed becomes progressively less certain the further it is projected (informational entropy). Both operate on economic exchange.

Our claim is that belief-dependency functions as a substitute for the direct observation of economic entropy, specifically at the points where that entropy cannot be observed by the parties present. A buyer does not need to believe a cow is valuable; its condition, and the entropy acting on it, are directly observable and will remain so as it ages. A holder of a fiat currency cannot observe the entropy acting on the issuing institution — policy risk, fiscal position, the accumulating base rate of currency failure across history — and so belief is substituted for that unavailable observation. Belief-dependency, on this account, is not a separate phenomenon from entropy; it is what fills the specific gap left where entropy exists but cannot be tracked directly.

This reframes monetary crisis. Hyperinflation episodes such as Weimar Germany (1923) and Zimbabwe (2008) are not anomalies internal to an otherwise well-functioning system. They are the discharge, all at once, of entropy that had been accumulating in an institution's fiscal and political position while belief — rather than direct observation — stood in for tracking it. The gap between what was believed and what had actually accumulated closes by force rather than by measurement.

This has a direct bearing on efficiency comparisons between agnostic and belief-dependent exchange, taken up formally in Section 7. Where such comparisons price only the immediate, visible costs of unmediated exchange (search, matching, coordination) against an implicitly zero cost on the belief-dependent side, they are comparing a priced cost to an unpriced one, not a real cost to no cost. The belief-dependent side's costs are not absent; under this account they are the specific entropy categories identified above, deferred rather than charged at the point of transaction: inflation as an unlevied tax on holding the instrument, counterparty and credit risk priced implicitly rather than disclosed, compliance and intermediation costs that are the visible edge of the administering class described in Section 5, and confiscation or freezing risk, which is the recruitment cost of the institutional register made concrete. None of these are hypothetical; each is measurable in principle, and each is routinely excluded from the standard efficiency case for money.

This gives applied economic agnosticism (Section 8) its operational content: a way of asking, of any transaction, what its belief-dependency actually conceals.

## 7. The Boundary Condition: Separation by Time and Distance

Zero belief-dependency is frequently assumed to fail in three areas of economic life: storage of value over long horizons, coordination at scale, and specialization of labor. We argue these are not three independent limits but a single constraint, restated three times.

| Apparent limit | Restated under the framework |
| --- | --- |
| Storage of value over time | A physical good's entropy (decay) is a fact of nature, not an artifact of belief-dependency. Money does not solve this; it substitutes a non-decaying *promise* for a decaying good, which conceals the entropy rather than eliminating it. |
| Coordination at scale | Scale itself does not require belief-dependency. A large number of zero-register trades can aggregate to significant scale with no single absent party required. What requires belief-dependency is *imposed* coordination — a single ledger or authority that all participants must trust simultaneously — which is a design choice, not a necessary condition of scale. |
| Specialization of labor | Specialization inside a dense, local, present market requires no belief: a producer trading directly with buyers who are physically present needs no trust in anyone absent. What fails at zero belief-dependency is specialization aimed at a buyer separated by time or distance — which is the storage and coordination problems recurring under a different name. |

Each apparent limit, on inspection, is the same boundary condition applied to a different object: physical goods (storage), coordination mechanisms (imposed scale), and labor allocation (specialization). The underlying constraint is narrower than it first appears: zero belief-dependency is achievable wherever a transaction is settled among parties who are present to one another and to the entropy governing what they exchange, and becomes structurally unachievable only where the transaction is separated from that entropy by time or distance. This is a considerably smaller concession than the conventional view — that large parts of economic life inherently require belief — implies.

This boundary condition also bears on the standard efficiency case for money. This paper does not dispute that unmediated exchange carries real, immediate search and matching costs; it disputes that the comparison built on them is complete, since the belief-dependent side's costs are deferred rather than absent (Section 6). It does not claim agnostic exchange wins that comparison in every case, only that the comparison has generally not been made in full.

## 8. Applied Economic Agnosticism as a Recognition Method

The preceding sections establish belief-dependency as a property of a transaction. This section defines applied economic agnosticism as a property of the *observer's* model of that transaction, and argues that the latter, not the former, is the operationally useful construct.

**Definition 3 (Applied economic agnosticism).** Applied economic agnosticism is the capacity to recognize, for any given exchange, (a) whether its completeness depends on a party not present, (b) which register — institutional, protocol, or none — that dependency occupies, and (c) whether the entropy that dependency conceals is in principle directly observable by the parties present, or has simply gone untracked.

**Corollary to Definition 3.** Recognition exercised once, on a single transaction, has limited diagnostic value: most transactions carry some belief-dependency, and knowing this of any one of them in isolation is rarely informative. The capacity becomes useful as a maintained practice — a standing habit of observation applied consistently across many transactions and over time — because it is the pattern across instances, not any single instance, that allows accumulating entropy (Section 6) to be seen before it discharges. This is the sense in which the framework is termed *applied* economic agnosticism rather than economic agnosticism as such: the contribution is not a taxonomy invoked once, but a discipline of observation maintained continuously, applicable in principle to any account of value — including chartalism (Section 9) — regardless of which register that account operates in. The lens does not compete with the accounts it is turned on; it reads what each one is asking its participants to believe, and for how long that belief has gone untested.

Under this definition, agnosticism is not a claim about which transactions are pure. A transaction that is fully belief-dependent, and correctly recognized as such by its participants, satisfies applied economic agnosticism in a stronger sense than a nominally direct trade whose hidden dependencies go unnoticed. The zero-register barter of Sections 3–4 is not agnostic *because* it involves a cow and a sack of wheat; it is agnostic because both parties can observe exactly what the trade does and does not depend on. Conversely, a currency transaction is belief-dependent in a dangerous sense specifically when its users have stopped being able to see that it is — when the entropy accumulating behind it (Section 6) is no longer being tracked by anyone in the exchange.

This reframing has a practical corollary. Applied economic agnosticism does not prescribe a return to barter, nor does it treat belief-dependency as a defect to be engineered away in every case; Section 7 establishes that some transactions cannot reach the zero register without abandoning function altogether. What it prescribes is a standing question to be asked of any exchange: *where is the entropy in this transaction, and can I observe it directly, or am I trusting an absent party to track it on my behalf?* This question is answerable at any register, including the institutional and protocol registers, and its answer does not by itself argue for exiting a belief-dependent system — only for knowing, accurately, that one is inside one.

## 9. Relation to Existing Monetary Theory

Chartalism, and Modern Monetary Theory in its contemporary form, ground a currency's value in the state's capacity to impose and enforce a tax liability payable only in its own token. In the terms of this paper, that is an institutional-register belief-dependency claim: value rests on trust in a state's continued capacity and willingness to enforce.

The belief-dependency axis developed here engages that account directly. A transaction settled at the zero register (Section 3) does not merely resist taxation; its completeness never depended on the state, or on any absent party, in the first place. A tax-driven-demand account of value applies, by construction, only within the institutional register. It says nothing about, and cannot reach, an exchange that never entered that register at all.

This narrows, rather than overturns, the chartalist claim. Chartalism remains a coherent account of why institutional-register currencies hold value. What it is not is a general account of value or exchange: it is an account of one register among three (Section 3), and the existence of a zero-belief-dependency register outside its reach follows directly from Definitions 1 and 2 rather than requiring separate argument.

## 10. Conclusion and Directions for Further Research

This paper has argued that money and barter are not sequential stages of a single institution's development but two structurally distinct modes of exchange, distinguished by belief-dependency: the degree to which a transaction's completeness relies on a party not present. Barter, correctly read against the anthropological record, is not a primitive baseline that money improved upon but the permanently available zero-point of this axis. Money's institutional and protocol registers substitute belief for the direct observation of economic entropy — decay, uncertainty, and risk that accumulate across time and distance — and monetary crisis is best understood as the discharge of entropy that belief had stopped tracking. What appear to be three separate limits on agnostic exchange — storage, coordination at scale, and specialization — reduce to a single boundary condition: separation of the transacting parties, in time or distance, from one another and from the entropy governing what they exchange. From this we derive applied economic agnosticism, defined not as a preference for barter but as a recognition capacity: the standing ability to identify a transaction's belief-dependency, its register, and the observability of the entropy it conceals.

This is presented as a first draft, and several extensions are left for further work. First, further work could examine how belief-dependency interacts with a transaction's observability to outside parties — a property addressed separately — which would likely yield a small number of distinct exchange types worth naming individually, with different practical and regulatory implications. Second, the entropy construct introduced in Section 6 is presented qualitatively; a more rigorous treatment — potentially borrowing directly from information theory — could specify entropy accumulation and discharge in monetary systems with greater precision, and could in principle generate testable predictions about the timing of belief-dependent system failures. Third, the boundary condition derived in Section 7 (separation by time and distance) implies design criteria for exchange systems seeking to minimize belief-dependency without sacrificing scale; a fuller treatment of imposed versus emergent coordination, and of the conditions under which the former can be replaced by the latter, is warranted. Finally, empirical work applying the recognition method of Section 8 to specific historical and contemporary monetary episodes would test whether the framework's central claim — that crisis follows from untracked entropy rather than from belief-dependency per se — holds beyond the illustrative cases discussed here.

## Annex A. A Break-Even Model: Direct Exchange vs. Bank Transfer for the Same Offer

**Setup.** A buyer wants to acquire one cow, valued by both parties at €800. Two ways to settle the same offer, at the same moment: direct exchange of two bags of grain judged equivalent to €800, handed over in person on the spot; or a bank transfer of €800 to the seller's account. Both are point-in-time settlements of the identical transaction — this is deliberate, to avoid comparing a single exchange against a holding period, which would conflate two different economic acts.

**The two totals.**

Direct exchange (grain for cow): total cost = *S*, where *S* is the cost of arranging this specific exchange — finding a seller willing to accept grain rather than money, negotiating an equivalence both parties accept, and transporting the grain. This is the double-coincidence-of-wants cost, made concrete rather than left as a term of art.

Bank transfer: total cost = *F* + *p* × *L*, where *F* is the explicit, priced cost of the transfer (bank fee, any FX spread), *p* is the probability that the transfer is disrupted at the point of settlement (flagged for review, technically failed, or the account frozen for an unrelated reason before the cow changes hands), and *L* is the cost if that happens — the transaction falls through, funds are held pending review, and the buyer must wait, dispute, or seek the cow elsewhere.

**Break-even condition.** Direct exchange is cheaper whenever *S* < *F* + *p* × *L*.

**Worked example 1 — routine settlement, low-friction jurisdiction.**

- *S* ≈ €30 (negotiation and transporting two bags of grain)
- *F* ≈ €5 (standard transfer fee)
- *p* ≈ 0.2% (illustrative — routine transfers rarely flagged or disrupted)
- *L* ≈ €400 (illustrative — cost of delay or dispute resolution if disrupted)
- *F* + *p*×*L* = €5 + (0.002 × €400) = **€5.80**

*S* (€30) > *F* + *p*×*L* (€5.80): the bank transfer is cheaper, matching ordinary experience — for a routine transaction with low disruption risk, money wins decisively.

**Worked example 2 — elevated settlement risk** (the transfer crosses a jurisdiction with active capital controls, or one party is under a compliance flag).

- *S* stays at €30 (arranging the grain exchange does not depend on banking conditions)
- *F* ≈ €5 (unchanged)
- *p* ≈ 5% (illustrative — meaningfully elevated disruption probability)
- *L* ≈ €800 (illustrative — full transaction value, if funds are frozen and the cow is sold elsewhere)
- *F* + *p*×*L* = €5 + (0.05 × €800) = **€45**

*S* (€30) < *F* + *p*×*L* (€45): direct exchange is now cheaper. The flip happens with *S* and *F* unchanged — only *p* and *L* moved, which is exactly the mechanism Section 4 describes historically: zero-register exchange reappears when disruption risk rises, not because preferences shift but because the arithmetic does.

**Variables this model excludes, stated openly.**

- *Divisibility and counterpart-matching asymmetry.* *S* is treated as a fixed cost for this transaction. In practice it rises sharply with transaction size, specificity, or scarcity of a matching counterpart — money's comparative advantage is precisely that it holds *S* near zero across almost any transaction, which this single-transaction model does not capture.
- *Risk aversion.* The model compares expected costs. A risk-averse party weights the low-probability, high-loss outcome (*L*) more heavily than its expectation *p*×*L*; a full treatment would apply a risk premium or certainty-equivalent adjustment rather than a bare expected value.
- *Network and reusability effects.* Money settled today remains usable for any future transaction; grain accepted today has value only insofar as the recipient can use or re-trade it. This model prices one transaction in isolation and does not capture that asymmetry.
- *p and L as illustrative, not measured.* The figures above are chosen for exposition. A rigorous application would ground *p* in historical disruption or dispute rates for the relevant payment rail and jurisdiction, and *L* in typical resolution costs or delay periods — both are in principle obtainable from published data, but are not sourced here.

Stating these openly is standard practice for a stylized model of this kind: its value is in making the trade-off structure explicit and computable, not in claiming to have priced every relevant variable.

**On the value of L.** Pressed on where the €800 figure in Worked example 2 comes from, the honest answer is that no single figure is defensible, and treating *L* as one number is itself a modeling error, not just an evidentiary gap.

*L* is not a point value but a probability-weighted mix of distinct failure modes:

- *Resolved with delay* (most common) — the transfer is flagged, held, and eventually clears. *L* here is the cost of the interruption itself: the price of a temporary substitute, spoilage or foregone use during the wait, and the time cost of managing the dispute. Typically small.
- *Resolved but repriced* — the buyer sources the asset elsewhere while waiting, and the original transfer clears afterward. *L* is the price gap between the original and replacement deal, plus friction.
- *Unresolved* — funds are permanently frozen, seized, or lost to fraud, with no recourse. *L* approaches the full transaction value plus the cost of re-sourcing. This is the worst case, not the typical one, and should carry a correspondingly small weight in *p*.

A defensible estimate of *p*×*L* is the probability-weighted sum across these regimes, anchored to real data rather than invented: bank SAR/AML flag rates and average resolution times, payment-processor chargeback and dispute timelines, and IMF or central-bank data on capital-control episodes for the elevated-risk case. Run against a plausible range drawn from these sources rather than a single chosen pair, the break-even condition still flips under stress — the qualitative result survives; only the precision of the illustrative numbers does not.

This difficulty is not incidental to the model — it is the point. *L* is hard to pin down to a single number because resolving a disrupted transfer means trusting an institutional process — a bank's compliance department, a regulator, a court — to eventually make the party whole. That dependency on a third party's discretion and timeline is the belief-dependency axis this paper defines in Section 2. Barter carries no equivalent *L*-tail not because the goods involved are special, but because there is no intermediary whose future cooperation is being wagered on. The unresolved question of what *L* should be is therefore not a weakness in the model to be patched with a better number — it is the belief-dependency axis, restated in the language of risk.
