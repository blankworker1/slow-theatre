# The Argument Nobody Made

Looking back, I'm fairly sure nobody at that table thought they were
witnessing the start of anything. I certainly didn't. It was a long
lunch, the kind that starts at one and is still going at six, laid
out under a vine that hadn't been pruned properly in years and gave
better shade for it. Twelve of us, maybe fourteen by the time the
last stragglers turned up. Someone's cousin had brought a guest of
their own, a stranger to the rest of us, who mostly sat at the far
end listening and didn't say much — I remember him more clearly now
than I did that day, for reasons that will make sense later.

The bread came from two different ovens, which was the whole problem.
One of us had baked far too much of it on purpose, and someone else
had brought a loaf from a bakery two towns over that everyone
insisted was better, and for a solid ten minutes the argument
occupied the whole table more thoroughly than anything of actual
consequence would all afternoon. It wasn't really an argument. Nobody
was trying to win. It was just something to do with our hands and our
opinions while the fish arrived.

It was somewhere in the middle of that — after the bread had been
settled by simply eating both, which I've always thought was the only
correct outcome — that he started talking about theatre.

Not seriously, at first. He'd been turning something over for weeks,
he said, some idea he wasn't sure was even an idea yet. He asked, of
nobody in particular, why nobody had ever done to a performance what
Petrini had done to a plate of food. Good. Clean. Fair. He said the
words the way you'd say something you'd already tested privately and
found, to your own surprise, still standing. A few people laughed,
not unkindly. Someone asked if he meant slow-motion theatre, actors
moving like they were underwater, and he laughed too and said no,
worse than that — he meant a show with no ticket, no season, no
curtain, built the same way this lunch had been built: nobody
running it, everybody bringing something, no bill at the end.

I remember thinking it sounded lovely and slightly mad in roughly
equal measure. I don't think I was alone in that. Someone at the far
end of the table — not the man himself, one of the others — pointed
out, not unkindly, that an audience needs somewhere to sit and a
reason to stay, and he agreed entirely, and said that was exactly the
problem he didn't yet know how to solve. He wasn't selling anything
that afternoon. That's the part I keep coming back to. He didn't
defend the idea when it was prodded. He just kept turning it over out
loud, the way you would a stone you'd picked up on a walk, uncertain
yet whether it was worth keeping.

What actually convinced me — and I don't think it was meant to
convince anyone, which is probably why it did — was watching him
describe the ending before he'd described almost anything else about
the middle. He said the whole thing should finish the way this lunch
would finish: everyone drifting toward a last glass together, no one
quite deciding when to leave, the details of who owed what to whom
having quietly stopped mattering hours ago. He hadn't worked out the
acts yet. He'd worked out the toast.

Someone asked what it would be called, and he said he didn't know
yet, and didn't seem bothered by not knowing, which struck me at the
time as either admirable or a warning sign, and I still can't tell
you which.

I've thought about that stranger at the far end of the table more
than once since — the guest of a guest, who I'm told wandered into a
piazza on some coast a couple of years later and stumbled onto a
truck and a table of secondhand things, and only worked out much
later where he'd first heard the words he was watching happen. I like
that he didn't know, that day at the lunch, what he was listening to.
None of us really did. We just argued about bread, ate both loaves,
and went home thinking we'd had a nice afternoon and heard a
half-formed idea we'd probably never hear about again.

I think about the bread a lot, actually. Two ovens, no vote, no
winner declared. We just ate both and moved on. It's a strange thing
to recognise, years later, as the first small rehearsal of everything
that came after — but I don't think it's a coincidence that the
place it all began was a table where nobody was in charge of who ate
what, and nobody thought to keep score.
