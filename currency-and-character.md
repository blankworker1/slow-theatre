# Currency and Character

Theatre inherited two devices from the world around it, and has used
them for so long that neither still looks like a choice.

The first is currency. A ticket is sold, a seat is bought, and the
transaction is complete before anyone has said a word to anyone
else. Money is very good at this: it lets two parties conclude their
business without needing to trust, know, or answer to one another as
people. The price stands in for the negotiation. Once it's paid, the
debt is discharged — there is nothing further owed, and nothing
further exposed.

The second is character. An actor steps onto a stage and stops being
answerable, personally, for what happens next. Whatever is said,
felt, confessed, or done belongs to the role. The audience judges
Hamlet's indecision, not the actor's own; weeps for a grief that
costs the performer nothing outside the two hours it's worn. The
mask does for a person's exposure what money does for a merchant's —
it absorbs the risk of being fully seen, and returns something
smaller and safer in its place: a character, rather than a price.

These are not two separate conveniences. They are the same
invention, aimed in two directions. Both are technologies of
substitution. Both let a party participate without being fully
present as themselves — one substitutes a number for a relationship,
the other substitutes a role for a person. And both work by the same
mechanism: something stands between two people so that neither has
to be fully answerable to the other. That's what a price does. It's
also what a mask does.

The efficiency this buys is real. A market cleared by price needs no
negotiation between strangers who'll never meet again; a stage
cleared by character needs no actor to risk their actual dignity
eight times a week. But the efficiency has a cost, and it's the same
cost twice: the thing being protected — the actor, the audience
member, the person behind the transaction — is also the thing being
withheld. Nobody in the exchange is actually, fully there. Money
makes presence optional economically. Character makes it optional
personally. Over enough repetition, both quietly hollow out
whatever they were first built to serve — a marketplace that forgets
the people trading in it, a theatre that forgets the people standing
in it.

Slow Theatre declines both substitutions, and treats them as one
decision rather than two. A performance with no price and a
performance with no character are not separate reforms answering
separate problems; they are the same refusal, applied to the two
places a person is usually allowed to hide. Remove the price, and
what a person offers has to be worth something on its own judgment,
face to face, with nothing standing in to answer for it. Remove the
character, and what a person says or promises has to be theirs, in
their own name, with nothing standing in to answer for it either.
Neither removal is complete without the other — a performance that
kept the mask but dropped the price would simply be free theatre; a
performance that kept the price but dropped the mask would simply be
an honest market. It's the two together that leave nobody, on
either side of anything that happens, with anywhere left to hide.

What's left, when both are gone, isn't a cheaper or more sincere
version of the same activity. It's a different one. Not a
transaction wearing a costume, and not a costume wearing a
transaction — just people, answerable, in full, for what they
actually do.
