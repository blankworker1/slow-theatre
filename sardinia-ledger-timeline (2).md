# The Ledger Has Always Been Elsewhere: A Timeline of Legibility in Sardinia

## Thesis

Across roughly 2,700 years of documented history, Sardinia has almost never been the author of its own economic and legal record. Every ruling power that has controlled the island — Phoenician, Roman, Byzantine, Aragonese, Spanish, Piedmontese, Italian, and now the EU — has arrived with, or built, an apparatus of legibility: a written language, a survey, a cadastre, a legal code, a directive — through which land, resources, and people become countable, taxable, and transferable. The stated rationale for that apparatus changes with every era (tribute, salvation, feudal order, Enlightenment property rights, national modernization, public safety, decarbonization), but the underlying structure does not: an authority based elsewhere defines the "public" or "higher" interest, produces the record that makes Sardinian land and resources visible to that interest, and extracts accordingly. Local custom is not usually erased outright — more often it survives underneath the imposed apparatus, tolerated as long as it doesn't obstruct extraction — until a technically sharper version of the ledger arrives that finally can dissolve it.

In this entire span there are only two clear exceptions, both clustered in the 11th–14th centuries, when the ledger and the land briefly spoke with the same voice.

## The timeline

### 1. Pre-literate Sardinia (Nuragic era, c. 1800–500 BCE)
No indigenous writing, no coinage. The Nuragic civilization is defined almost entirely by material remains — nuraghi, bronzes, tombs — because it never generated its own textual record. It has a collective land-tenure practice (later called *ademprivio*) that will outlast every subsequent conquest for the next 2,300 years. Even the name "Nuragic" is an external, modern label; the people never named themselves.

### 2. First external ledger (9th century BCE onward)
The **Nora Stone** (c. 825 BCE), the oldest Phoenician inscription outside the Levant, is also the first written appearance of the name "Sardinia." Its content — conquest, temple politics, or both — is still disputed, which is itself telling: the very first sentence written about the island is ambiguous and authored by a trading colony, not by Sardinians. Punic administration follows with cities, tribute, and commerce; its actual paper records (largely perishable) don't survive, but the apparatus — coastal city-states, controlled trade, a fortified inland *limes* — is well attested.

### 3. Roman and Byzantine layers (238 BCE–c. 700s CE)
Seven centuries of Roman provincial taxation and grain extraction, followed by Vandal disruption, then Byzantine reconquest (534 CE) that is administratively still "Rome," just governed from Constantinople. No physical tax ledger from either period survives on the island; what we have is Latin literature, papal correspondence complaining of "Byzantine misgovernment," and law. The end of Byzantine control is undatable — it simply thins out into local self-rule as imperial attention is consumed elsewhere.

### 4. The first exception: the condaghes (11th–13th c.)
As the giudicati (Cagliari, Arborea, Torres, Gallura) fill the post-Byzantine vacuum, monasteries begin keeping **condaghes** — land and transaction registers — in *Sardinian*, not Latin or Greek. The word itself is a fossil of the departed Byzantine administration (*kontákion*, the scroll-pole), but for the first time the *content* is authored locally and in the vernacular. This is the earliest instance in the record of the ledger and the governed population speaking the same language.

### 5. The second exception: the Carta de Logu (1392)
Eleanor of Arborea's legal code — written in Sardinian, personally drafted by a sovereign ruler rather than imposed by an outside power — protects women's property rights and explicitly criminalizes forest destruction, river pollution, and the hunting of breeding animals. It is the single moment in this entire history where the island's own ledger *protects* rather than extracts. It survives Arborea's own collapse: Aragon, after conquering the island in 1420, keeps the Carta de Logu in force for four centuries (until 1827) — administrative apparatus outliving the sovereignty that produced it, run now on behalf of a foreign crown.

### 6. Aragonese and Spanish rule (1420–1720)
Feudal ownership is transplanted wholesale from Iberia; the Cagliari state archive is founded in 1332 and — per a single, not independently corroborated source — formally declared crown patrimony in 1618; even setting that specific date aside, control of the record becomes an explicit instrument of sovereignty across this period. No new indigenous fiscal register emerges in this period; the Carta de Logu persists only as the civil/criminal layer beneath an externally imposed feudal ownership structure.

### 7. The Editto delle Chiudende (1820)
Conceived in Turin (tracing to Piedmontese minister Bogino's 1759–73 reform program), signed by a Piedmontese king, published in Italian, and administered by Piedmontese-appointed viceroys, the Enclosures Edict authorizes the conversion of *ademprivio* — collective land use unbroken since Nuragic times — into private, enclosable title. No prior regime, across 2,300 years of occupation, had possessed a legal technology precise enough to fully dissolve the commons. Enlightenment-era property law and cadastral precision finally supply it. Effect: land concentrates in the hands of local elites (the *prinzipales*) and outside capital, not the pastoral majority. Resistance is repressed militarily (1832) and recurs as the Su Connottu revolt in 1868 — "the already-known," a demand to restore the pre-1820 order.

### 8. Deforestation (1820–1883, peak 1847)
The same instrument applied to timber: forests are first legally converted to state/demanial land, then sold to private, often speculative buyers. Piedmontese surveyors map the resource before it is stripped — legibility as reconnaissance for extraction, explicitly framed as serving "superior national interest." One forestry-history account (Caterini) puts forest cover at over 500,000 hectares entering the century and under 100,000 at its end — a figure sourced from a single interview rather than a cross-verified dataset, and one that implies substantial 20th-century recovery given current true-forest cover is again close to 500,000 hectares; the directional claim of severe deforestation is well corroborated across sources, the precise low-point figure is not. Protective legislation (1877) arrives decades after the damage is largely done.

### 9. Dams, reclamation, and eminent domain (1897–1962)
Water is nationalized through the same rationale-shift pattern: dam-building (Tirso/Lake Omodeo, Flumendosa, Coghinas) is financed and engineered entirely off-island — the original 1918–1924 Tirso construction capitalized by Banca Commerciale Italiana through the Società Imprese Idrauliche ed Elettriche del Tirso, with the shareholding transferred only in 1933 to IRI (founded that year, Rome-based) — justified as agricultural modernization and public health. The 1923–25 flooding of Zuri for Lake Omodeo shows the ledger's exact limits: the law compensates what it can classify and value — a named medieval church is dismantled and rebuilt stone by stone — while the submerged Nuragic nuraghe, tombs, and the land itself receive no equivalent recognition. Postwar land reform (ETFAS) finally redistributes reclaimed land to those who work it, but only after decades of state administration of land created by prior expropriation. The 1962 nationalization of the electricity sector (ENEL) folds Sardinia's rivers into a single Rome-headquartered balance sheet.

### 10. Green energy (present day)
The rationale is now decarbonization, and the authority is now EU climate targets cascaded through Rome to the region: Sardinia is assigned a fixed MW quota it must absorb by 2030. Sardinia's own attempt to write its *own* legibility — the 2024 "Aree Idonee" law, defining unsuitable zones for large renewable installations — is struck down by the Constitutional Court; developers then win in administrative courts. Ministries in Rome split on approvals; the regional government is present at the final decision only as an invited party. The vocabulary is new (transition, sustainability, public interest) but the structural grievance is identical to every prior chapter: the body assessing "the public good" is not the body bearing the cost of it.

## The constant and the variable

| Era | Instrument | Stated rationale | Who authored the ledger |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nuragic | — (none) | — | No ledger; pure custom |
| Punic/Roman/Byzantine | Tribute, census, provincial law | Empire, salvation, order | Foreign administration |
| Condaghes (11th–13th c.) | Vernacular monastic registers | Church record-keeping | **Sardinian** |
| Carta de Logu (1392) | Sovereign legal code | Justice, stability, protection of land | **Sardinian** |
| Aragonese/Spanish | Feudal grants, royal archive | Dynastic possession | Foreign administration |
| Chiudende (1820) | Enclosure/property law | Enlightenment modernization | Piedmontese state |
| Deforestation (1820–83) | Demanial reclassification + sale | National interest, public order | Piedmontese state |
| Dams/reclamation (1897–1962) | Eminent domain, nationalization | Public health, industrial development | Roman/national state |
| Renewables (present) | EU/national siting targets | Decarbonization | EU via Roman state |

What varies across every row is the rationale and the specific technology of the ledger. What does not vary is the geography of authorship: with the sole exception of a roughly 250-year window in the medieval period, the entity deciding what Sardinian land and resources are *for* has always been headquartered somewhere else — and each successive version of the ledger has been technically sharper than the last, closing gaps that the previous regime's apparatus left open. The chiudende could do what Rome, Byzantium, and Spain never managed: end the commons outright. The 20th-century state could do what the chiudende could not: submerge whole valleys under a reservoir with full legal sanction. The EU-scale renewable-energy apparatus can now assign a binding numerical quota to the island's own landscape from a jurisdiction Sardinia does not sit within.

The single throughline worth holding onto: legibility has almost never been a neutral act of record-keeping in this history. It has been the precondition for extraction, refined generation after generation — and the rare moments it wasn't (the condaghes, the Carta de Logu) are the moments the record was written by the people who lived on the land it described.
