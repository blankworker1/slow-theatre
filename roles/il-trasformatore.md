# Il Trasformatore

*A satellite role of Slow Theatre / La Prova. Act Two only — an autonomous mini-stage running alongside the pedestal, present on some days, absent on others, never required for the gallery to function.*

---

## I. Historical context

Live-made objects have their own tradition, distinct from the slow crafts: the balloon artist working a fairground crowd, the street sculptor finishing a piece from scrap in the time it takes to draw a breath, the busker whose whole act is watching something be made rather than admiring it finished. The appeal isn't permanence — it's witnessed speed, the visible fact of a hand turning nothing into something while you stand there. Waste art and upcycling performance borrow this same energy for a different material: not balloons or ice, but the things a house clearance turns up — broken, discarded, headed for a skip until someone with a Dremel and a glue gun decides otherwise.

Il Trasformatore comes from that lineage, by way of FREMKT's Transformation Station — a live conversion act built around exactly this pace: fast, visible, one item at a time, an artist working in front of an audience, not a workshop and not a repair session. What FREMKT proved is that this kind of speed is its own legitimate register of care, not a lesser one. Slow Theatre's other satellites — the Remembrancer's yearly binding, La Sarta's treadle machine — measure attention in hours or a year. Il Trasformatore measures it in minutes, and the contrast is left standing rather than resolved: both are real work, watched at whatever pace it actually takes.

## II. The performance layer

Il Trasformatore works one act only, with no presence in Act One and no function in Act Three. Within Act Two, it runs as its own mini-stage — a corner of the square with its own tools, its own rhythm, nobody cued to gather round it. People drift over on their own schedule, the same low-threshold entry as everything else in the piece.

The material comes primarily from the table: what arrived in Act One as someone's unwanted rubbish is, wherever possible, what gets transformed live at the Station, kept visibly rather than replaced with an outside supply. This is the one rule that isn't inherited from FREMKT's own version — FREMKT expects a good artist to bring their own box and treats the floor as inspiration rather than primary material. La Prova inverts that default on purpose: without it, the thread connecting a gifted object to its life as art would quietly break, and that thread is the entire reason the Station exists.

Modification, joining, and finishing happen in view — cutting, shaping, fast assembly, whatever gets an object from rubbish to recognisable art in the time an audience will actually stay watching. The finishing move is the stencil: the same physical mark used across the project, branded onto the object at the exact moment it's done. From that instant the piece needs no further explanation. It carries its own proof of transformation, and it is, from that moment, barterable — there is no held-back portfolio piece the way La Sarta sometimes keeps one back for later. Everything Il Trasformatore finishes goes straight to the pedestal.

## III. What it completes

Act One's table was redesigned to let every object leave by one of several routes, decided privately by whoever takes it — useful as it is, resold for cash through Il Commerciante, or destined to become art. Of those routes, art was the one with no visible mechanism: an object simply reappeared later on a pedestal, transformed off-stage, unwitnessed. Il Trasformatore gives that route a place and a performance, the same way Il Commerciante gave the cash route a place, off to the side, without either mechanism becoming load-bearing. Act Two's actual main stage stays exactly what it has always been — the pedestal, the gallery, the barter. On a day Il Trasformatore doesn't appear, nothing about that changes; art still reaches the pedestal however it always did. What the role adds, when it's there, is visibility for one route among several — never a stage every object has to pass through.

## IV. Requirements

Basic tools for cutting, shaping, and fast assembly — a Dremel or equivalent, a hot glue gun, paint, and the stencil itself. No rehearsal beyond the craft, no fixed brief for what gets made, no cue to wait for. Whoever fills this role decides on the day what's worth transforming and how quickly, the same judgement every other satellite in Slow Theatre is trusted to make for itself. The only discipline that matters is working, visibly, from what the table actually gave that day — everything else is left to speed, skill, and whatever the crowd happens to be watching.
